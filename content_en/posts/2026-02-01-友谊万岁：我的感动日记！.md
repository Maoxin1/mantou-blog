**Life without friends must be dull. That thought has been with me for the past six months, and its voice keeps getting louder.**

I still remember how little of a social life I had during my four years at university. Whenever I had time, I went to the library and stayed all day. When I felt particularly bottled up, I would work through my WeChat contacts, “bothering” friends one by one and asking them to talk. Over time, that did not work out very well. I often reached out only when I needed somewhere to put my feelings, and I think they began to find it tiring. Perhaps I chose bad moments. Perhaps I was simply poor at noticing how they felt and caused them more trouble than I realized.

Gradually, I learned to be alone. At the time, that seemed like a clever solution: I thought I had dealt with my loneliness. Looking back, I do not think I had. After graduating, encountering more of the world, and spending time with more people, I began to see that my unmet emotional needs had never really gone away. They had taken another form. I kept emphasizing my strengths to distance myself from what I imagined to be the “ignorant crowd.” I can now see how harmful that was. It made my thinking closed and isolated—something that became particularly clear as I made new friends.

First, **I realized that I was not nearly as good at many things as I had imagined. In some areas, I could barely give myself a passing grade.** After almost a year of teaching myself Japanese, for example, I still could not manage even an ordinary conversation with a Japanese speaker. Second, many friends were not following the same path as me, yet **their openness, cheerfulness, humility, and courtesy** were exactly what I was slowly losing. **Those qualities are fundamental to getting along with people; they have value in themselves.** Third, **I began to see that happiness does not require great wealth. A loving family and healthy, safe loved ones can make an ordinary life a deeply good one.**

Last night, a young friend who is still in primary school gave me a small present: **a little frog she had made by hand.** I felt an unexpected burst of joy when she handed it to me, and that feeling grew after I got home. Sitting on my bed, I happily held the little frog, placed it beside my pillow, and took a picture to share on WeChat Moments. Then the excitement softened into a deeper happiness. I kept looking at that frog, unable to put the feeling aside.

![The little handmade frog](/images/posts/2026-02-01-1.jpg)

**Yes, this is how our feelings work.**

**They need attention, care, and some measure of fulfillment.**

**But perhaps they do not ask for much. A little—even a very little—can keep someone happy for a long, long time.**
