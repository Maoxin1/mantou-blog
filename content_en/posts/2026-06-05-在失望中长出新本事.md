Yesterday my phone froze, shut down after about ten seconds, and would not turn on again. I tried several ways to force a restart. A repair shop diagnosed the motherboard—the most troublesome and expensive possibility. I decided to get a second opinion from an official Samsung service center the next day.

After a quick breakfast, I took the bus there. They confirmed the diagnosis. “How much to repair it?” I asked. “More than 4,800 yuan.” I was stunned. They suggested an outside shop. I left feeling as if I had received bad news from a hospital.

When I returned to ask where else I might go, I was sent back to a technician. I understood his proposal as valuing my old phone at about 1,800 yuan if I traded it in, using that to offset a revised repair figure of roughly a third of the first quote, and paying more than 9,300 yuan for a new phone after a 500-yuan discount. The figures and the conversation left me confused and disappointed. After half an hour, I chose to look elsewhere. With a friend’s help, I had the problem fixed at a more reasonable price. I do not know how another store would have handled it; this one let me down.

![Phone screen during Fiat24 account registration](/images/posts/1000096122.jpg)

The episode drew my attention back to security. Yesterday I urgently registered for a card I hoped to use more safely for AI payments and other needs, only to find today that it had already been closed. The day before, I had applied for a Charles Schwab account, hoping to preserve a safer route for future US stock investing. These are my responses to risks I now see more clearly.

The past twenty-four hours have also made me think about information security. Are the passwords protecting my important accounts and money strong enough? How exposed is my personal information? Should I have a second phone in case the only one I own fails again?

Tomorrow I will take the time to review these questions. The theme is **security**.
