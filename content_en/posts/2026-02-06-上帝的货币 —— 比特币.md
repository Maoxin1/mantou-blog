> **If a currency could work without placing its rules in any one person’s hands, it might look less like an ordinary human institution.**

## Introduction: A different kind of money

Before talking about Bitcoin, I want to set its price aside. If I can discuss it only when its price moves, I am discussing speculation rather than the monetary system.

Most money systems depend on people and institutions making decisions: how much to issue, when to change policy, and what to do in a crisis. Bitcoin tries another approach. It sets rules in software and lets participants operate the network. It does not promise a stable price or wise decisions. The striking idea is to reduce the need for a central manager.

## 1. Bitcoin came from an era, not a lone flash of genius

### Cryptography reached ordinary users

Timothy C. May opened *The Crypto Anarchist Manifesto* with an image of a specter haunting the modern world. By the 1990s, cryptographic tools were reaching civilian users more widely. For cypherpunks, the question was who could communicate privately as the internet expanded the ability to monitor people.

Eric Hughes wrote in his 1993 *Cypherpunk’s Manifesto* that privacy mattered in an open society and should not be expected as a gift from large institutions. (<https://www.activism.net/cypherpunk/manifesto.html>) I take the lesson to be that privacy needs tools people can use, not only promises from those in power. The same question can be asked about money.

### Earlier experiments

David Chaum’s DigiCash pioneered anonymous digital payment using blind signatures. It depended, however, on a company to issue and clear payments; that company went bankrupt in 1998. Other proposals, including b-money, Bit Gold, and Hashcash, contributed ideas about decentralized records and proof of work. None became a self-sustaining currency network on Bitcoin’s scale. The challenge was not only clever technology but coordinating participants over time.

## 2. What is the more basic reason?

Scarcity, decentralization, and security all matter, but each points to the question I care about most: can a monetary network keep working without requiring users to place all trust in a particular intermediary? Bitcoin is an ambitious attempt to do so. “Trustless” does not mean that no judgment is ever needed; it means the rules can be checked and followed without giving one institution complete discretion.

The Bitcoin white paper describes a peer-to-peer form of electronic cash that can send online payments directly between parties without routing them through a financial institution. (<https://bitcoin.org/bitcoin.pdf>) The important part, for me, is the removal of that required intermediary.

## 3. Why minimizing trust matters

People change. Organizations have incentives. Institutions may make exceptions when their survival or other goals are at stake. None of this requires believing that every person is bad. It is enough to notice that a promise by a manager and a rule enforced by a broad network are different arrangements.

Bitcoin’s design has limits: users still rely on software, hardware, information, and their own ability to keep keys safe. The protocol can also change if participants adopt new rules. Yet it places fewer monetary decisions in any single person’s hands than a centrally managed currency does. That difference is what interests me.

## 4. What would count as failure?

A price crash, volatility, or public criticism would not by themselves prove that the underlying idea has failed. I would call the idea unnecessary if society no longer valued a monetary system with this kind of independence from a central operator. I cannot know whether that will happen. As long as power and information remain unevenly distributed, I think the experiment has a reason to exist.

## 5. Why call it “God’s currency”?

The title is a metaphor. Bitcoin has no CEO and no routine committee able to change its rules by decree. Satoshi Nakamoto stepped away. To me, the system can feel closer to a fixed set of constraints than to a company policy—though it is still a human-made protocol, maintained by people and capable of change through adoption.

That is also the irony I see at the end. One person steadily buys Bitcoin; another repeatedly bets on leveraged contracts. The first approach can leave room to make mistakes and continue. The second may demand being right at precisely the wrong moment. What matters is not only what I buy, but what kind of error my method allows me to survive.
